Night 154

Gag Is Not Choke

Month 6: The Feast Begins

Day four. A soft lump wandered too far back, and Olivia gagged: cough, splutter, red face, the tongue shoving it forward. Four seconds. The lump came back to the front. She grinned, damp, and opened for more.

Mama had done the hardest thing in feeding, which is nothing. She stayed level and watchful and let it run.

In the plainest type this book owns: gagging is loud, choking is quiet.

The gag is the guardian working, set far forward on a baby's tongue for this season, and it goes off often and harmlessly while the mouth learns its new job. A choke is the opposite country: silence, or a wrong small sound, no cry, the face wide-eyed or going dusky, nothing moving. A gag wants a calm witness. A choke wants action, that instant, from hands that took the infant first-aid class before the feast began. Every adult in this house who will ever hold the spoon takes that class.

Table rules keep gags mere gags: seated upright, strapped in, watched, never hurried, no laughing with a mouthful, no eating on the move. And the forbidden list stays posted: whole grapes, whole nuts, popcorn, hard round things of any kind, kept off her plate until she is four, and grapes quartered lengthwise even then.

The guardian went off twice more. Mama stayed level each time, hands in her lap.
