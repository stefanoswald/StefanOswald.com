Night 45

Sing Anyway

Month 2: Smiles and Sleep

Papa could not sing. This was not modesty. At his cousin's wedding a kind woman had asked him to hum a little quieter, and he had been humming. His whistling was worse. The family did not discuss it.

But a strange thing happens when a baby comes. The audience changes, and this audience had one question for a singer: is it you? It was, every time. So he sang. The burping song, an original. The socks song, to the tune of something he had ruined years ago. The evening news, in opera, holding a wooden spoon.

Olivia watched him the way a sunflower watches the sun.

There is a reason to do this beyond the pleasure of it. Songs stretch the vowels, repeat, and glue words to a beat. A small brain likes its language served that way. Every place that has babies has lullabies. Off-key delivers all of it. Pitch is for concert halls. Babies listen for warmth, and warmth was the one note Papa could always hit.

"He's flat," said Mama from the doorway.

"She's dancing," said Papa.

She was not dancing. She had no dancing yet. But her feet were going, in something near enough to time to count, and when the spoon came down on the last note both feet stopped, and she waited to see if there was more.
